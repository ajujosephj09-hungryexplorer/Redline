# Clause Types by Complaint Frequency and Real Harm
**Research by Agent 2 | Sources: FTC, CFPB, EPI, Consumer Reports, class-action records**

Ranked by evidence of frequency in actual complaints and enforcement actions, not theoretical risk.

---

## 1. Forced Arbitration + Class Action Waiver
**Frequency:** Highest documented reach — at least 826.5 million consumer arbitration agreements in force as of 2018; 56.2% of private-sector non-union employees (60+ million workers) subject to forced employment arbitration; 81 of Fortune 100 companies use arbitration clauses for consumer transactions, 78 of which include class action waivers.

**Real consequence:** Consumers file only ~411 arbitration cases/year across six major financial product markets despite tens of millions being covered. Consumers achieve relief in only 9% of arbitration disputes. When companies counter-sue, arbitrators favor them 93% of the time and order consumers to pay an average of $7,725. Workers drop harassment, wrongful-termination, and discrimination claims rather than navigate the private tribunal system.

**Source:** https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers

---

## 2. Auto-Renewal / Negative Option Billing
**Frequency:** Triggered dozens of federal and state enforcement actions; 34 state AGs coordinated settlements against one company alone; FTC sued Amazon, Uber, Match.com, Chegg, and Cleo AI. California's Automatic Renewal Taskforce logged nearly 200,000 instances of post-cancellation billing by Chegg alone since 2020.

**Real consequence:** Noom ordered to pay $56 million; McAfee settled for up to $80 million; Thrive Market paid $1.55 million in penalties and restitution; LifeLock settled for $2.5 million. Core harm: consumers charged for services they cancelled or never knowingly agreed to renew.

**Source:** https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/a-dozen-new-lawsuits-and-two-7-5m-settlements-signal-a-new-era-for-automatic-renewal-compliance/

**Supporting source:** https://classactionu.org/class-actions/auto-renewals/

---

## 3. Non-Compete Clause
**Frequency:** Affects an estimated 30–60 million U.S. private-sector workers (roughly 1 in 5). FTC brought enforcement actions against specific employers (Rollins, pet cremation companies, security guard firms) for applying non-competes to low-wage workers without individualized consideration. FTC launched a formal Request for Information in 2025 after its 2024 ban was struck down.

**Real consequence:** Workers cannot change jobs in their field without risking litigation. FTC estimates that banning non-competes would add $250–$296 billion/year to worker earnings and close racial and gender wage gaps by 3.6–9.1%. Low-wage workers are the most vulnerable — security guards and hourly workers have been subject to the same restrictive clauses as executives.

**Source:** https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition

**Supporting source:** https://www.nelp.org/insights-research/faq-on-non-compete-agreements/

## 4. Indemnification Clause (Overbroad / One-Sided)
**Frequency:** Cited as one of the most common sources of commercial litigation disputes; frequently flagged in construction, technology, and vendor contracts. Small businesses are the primary victims because they sign large-company standard form agreements without negotiation leverage.

**Real consequence:** A subcontractor who signs a broad "any and all claims arising out of the project" indemnity clause may be forced to pay defense costs, damages, and settlements for accidents caused entirely by the general contractor's negligence. Indemnity clauses can require payment of the other party's attorney fees — costs that can exceed the value of the original contract.

**Source:** https://mhslaw.com/the-hidden-danger-in-your-contracts-indemnification-clauses-that-can-sink-a-small-business/

**Supporting source:** https://lynchlaw-group.com/contract-issues-for-small-business-owners-starting-with-indemnification/

---

## 5. Liability Cap (Below Actual Damage Potential)
**Frequency:** Standard in virtually all vendor/SaaS/technology contracts; flagged as a high-risk clause in contract review guides and vendor agreement analyses. Courts regularly adjudicate disputes where liability caps exclude consequential damages (lost profits, business interruption) that represent the bulk of actual harm.

**Real consequence:** A vendor causes a data breach or service outage that costs a business $2 million in lost revenue, but the liability cap is limited to fees paid in the prior month (often a few thousand dollars). Caps that also exclude consequential damages can leave the harmed party with no meaningful recovery whatsoever.

**Source:** https://flag.red/contract-guides/limitation-of-liability-in-vendor-agreement

**Supporting source:** https://contrivox.com/blog/limitation-of-liability-clause-explained

---

## 6. Non-Disparagement / Gag Clause
**Frequency:** Sufficiently widespread that Congress passed the Consumer Review Fairness Act (CRFA) in 2017 to ban them in consumer form contracts. The FTC brought its first enforcement-only CRFA actions in May 2019 against three businesses and issued additional actions in June 2019.

**Real consequence:** Consumers who posted negative reviews faced penalties and threatened lawsuits. HVAC companies, flooring firms, and service providers all used gag clauses to suppress legitimate consumer feedback, preventing other consumers from making informed decisions. Under the CRFA, violating companies face FTC penalties.

**Source:** https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2019/06/ftc-announces-two-actions-enforcing-consumer-review-fairness-act

**Supporting source:** https://www.allaboutadvertisinglaw.com/2019/06/ftc-continues-to-clamp-down-on-gag-clauses.html

---

## 7. IP Assignment (Overbroad)
**Frequency:** Consistently flagged as the top red flag in freelance and independent contractor contracts. Multiple dedicated legal guides identify it as the most consequential clause for creators, developers, and designers.

**Real consequence:** A designer signs a contract with no explicit IP exclusion; the clause assigns "all work product created during the engagement" — which courts may interpret to include the designer's pre-existing portfolio tools, templates, or style guides. The designer is then legally barred from reusing their own prior work for other clients, effectively destroying their business model. Similarly, startups using contractors without clear IP assignment later discover they don't own their own codebase.

**Source:** https://flag.red/contract-guides/ip-assignment-in-freelance-agreement

**Supporting source:** https://patentpc.com/blog/how-to-prevent-ip-ownership-disputes-with-contractors-and-freelancers

---

## 8. Fee Escalator / Price Escalation Clause
**Frequency:** Common in construction, SaaS/software, and long-term service contracts. Buyers routinely report surprise when "fixed price" contracts include buried escalation language. Escalator clauses frequently appear in pricing exhibits, order forms, or addenda incorporated by reference — documents that get skimmed, not read.

**Real consequence:** Buyers sign what they believe is a fixed-price construction contract and receive a bill for significantly more after material costs rise. In software contracts, CPI-linked escalators can compound annually, raising a $100K/year contract to $130K+ within three years with no renegotiation right. Disputes arise when trigger conditions (e.g., "material cost increase") are vague and parties disagree on whether the clause applies.

**Source:** https://berlinpatten.com/beware-the-escalation-clause-a-hidden-pitfall-in-new-construction-contracts/

**Supporting source:** https://www.pocsheet.com/blog/price-increase-clause-cpi-escalation

---

## Methodology Notes
- Searches conducted: 11 web searches, 5 pages read
- Ranking basis: enforcement action frequency, documented complaint volume, settlement dollar amounts, and number of workers/consumers affected
- Theoretical risks without documented complaints or enforcement were excluded or ranked lower
- Unilateral termination clauses were investigated but lacked sufficient complaint-frequency evidence relative to the 8 clauses above; they are an emerging area of vendor dispute litigation but not yet backed by the same complaint volume
